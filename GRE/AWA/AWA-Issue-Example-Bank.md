# GRE Issue Essay — Example Bank, Themes & Prompt Mapping

> Personal notes on examples, themes, and argument patterns for GRE Issue Essays. The aim is to build a flexible set of examples that can be adapted across a wide range of prompts while maintaining clear reasoning and relevant analysis.

## PART 1: THE 25 EXAMPLES

### E01 — Vaccine Maitri (2021)
**Category:** Government / International
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** During the COVID-19 pandemic, India launched the "Vaccine Maitri" (Vaccine Friendship) initiative, exporting over 72 million vaccine doses to 90+ developing and neighboring countries (such as Bhutan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and multiple African nations), often as outright grants. Even while facing intense domestic criticism during the second wave for exporting vaccines before immunizing its own population, the government stood by its global commitments. This strategic generosity strengthened India's soft power, stabilized regional health, and positioned the nation as a reliable global leader in crisis response.

**Core Lesson:** Strategic generosity and international aid, even at a high short-term domestic cost, build invaluable long-term diplomatic goodwill, soft power, and regional stability.

**Counter-Lesson:** Prioritizing international aid over immediate domestic crises can cause severe political backlash and raise ethical issues if the home population is in urgent need.

**Use For:** Government spending, international cooperation, long-term thinking, foreign aid, crisis response, public policy, nationalism vs. globalism.

### E02 — M. S. Swaminathan & the Green Revolution
**Category:** Science / Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In the 1960s, India was facing severe food shortages, relying on food imports under the US PL-480 program ("ship-to-mouth" existence) and facing predictions of mass famine. Geneticist M. S. Swaminathan collaborated with M. S. Swaminathan to introduce high-yield, disease-resistant dwarf wheat varieties, combining them with local agricultural practices. He pushed the government to build grain silos, guarantee crop pricing, and train farmers. Within a decade, India doubled its wheat production, achieved food self-sufficiency, and averted catastrophic famines, transforming from a food-deficit to a food-surplus nation.

**Core Lesson:** Applied science, combined with public policy and structural agricultural reform, can solve catastrophic social crises (like famine) and secure national self-reliance.

**Counter-Lesson:** Rapid, intensive technological shifts in agriculture can lead to long-term environmental degradation, soil depletion, excessive groundwater usage, and socioeconomic disparities among small farmers.

**Use For:** Science solving problems, public funding, individual impact, agriculture, technology for social good, unintended consequences of technology.

### E03 — Alan Turing & Enigma Codebreaking
**Category:** Science / Technology
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** During WWII, Alan Turing cracked Nazi Germany's "unbreakable" Enigma code by conceptualizing early computing machines and applying abstract mathematics to a practical crisis. His interdisciplinary approach fundamentally shortened the war by an estimated two years and saved millions of lives. Furthermore, as an eccentric outsider who faced severe persecution, his story proves that groundbreaking innovation often comes from unconventional thinkers who challenge established methods.

**Core Lesson:** Groundbreaking innovation often requires interdisciplinary thinking and unconventional outsiders challenging established norms.

**Counter-Lesson:** Pure intellect and technological advancement do not guarantee personal acceptance or ethical treatment by society.

**Use For:** Science, innovation, outsiders, war, technology, interdisciplinary work, genius, challenging authority.

### E04 — Shri Mahila Griha Udyog Lijjat Papad (1959)
**Category:** Social Reform / Business
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In 1959, seven semi-literate Gujarati women living in a crowded tenement in Mumbai borrowed Rs 80 to start a papad-making business, aiming to earn a sustainable livelihood. They established a cooperative model where every female employee was a co-owner ("bhagini") and profit-sharer. The cooperative refused charity and focused on consistent quality, decentralizing the labor (women rolled papads at home to balance domestic work) while centralizing quality control. Today, Lijjat Papad employs over 45,000 women, exports globally, and is a multi-million-dollar self-sustaining enterprise that successfully lifted thousands of families out of poverty.

**Core Lesson:** Grassroots cooperative business models that empower marginalized individuals can eradicate poverty and challenge traditional corporate structures while remaining financially self-sustaining.

**Counter-Lesson:** Maintaining decentralized quality control and cooperative principles becomes highly complex and sluggish when scaling to a national or international level.

**Use For:** Social responsibility, business, poverty, innovation, individual impact, cooperation over competition, grassroots reform.

### E05 — Steve Jobs & Apple
**Category:** Business / Innovation
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** Steve Jobs transformed multiple industries—including personal computing, music, and telecommunications—by obsessing over design, user experience, and aesthetic perfection rather than just technical functionality. His insistence that technology should be intuitive and beautiful resulted in the iPhone, which redefined modern life. His ouster and triumphant return to Apple underline that true visionary leadership requires a balance of uncompromising creative vision and effective execution.

**Core Lesson:** Visionary leadership and an uncompromising focus on user experience often triumph over pure technical utility.

**Counter-Lesson:** Autocratic and demanding leadership styles can alienate colleagues, showing the friction between individual genius and teamwork.

**Use For:** Innovation, leadership, business, technology, vision, creative thinking, competition, design.

### E06 — Mahatma Gandhi & the Salt March (1930)
**Category:** Social Reform / Leadership
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In 1930, to protest the British colonial monopoly and heavy tax on salt (a basic necessity for every human), Mahatma Gandhi launched the Salt Satyagraha by walking 240 miles from Sabarmati to Dandi. Upon reaching the coast, he picked up a lump of natural salt, technically breaking the colonial law. This simple, deeply symbolic act of civil disobedience was easily understood by the masses, including the illiterate poor. It triggered nationwide peaceful protests, resulted in the arrest of over 60,000 people, and successfully shattered the myth of British moral authority, drawing global media attention and catalyzing the broader independence movement.

**Core Lesson:** A simple, highly symbolic, and principled act of civil disobedience can unify a diverse population, expose institutional injustice, and shatter the authority of a powerful government.

**Counter-Lesson:** Symbolic civil disobedience requires extensive grassroots organizational backing and strategic timing; otherwise, it can lead to unstructured chaos or brutal state suppression without achieving legislative change.

**Use For:** Civil disobedience, individual action, moral conviction, social reform, challenging unjust authority, mass motivation, peaceful protest.

### E07 — Apollo 11 Moon Landing
**Category:** Government / Science
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Fulfilling President Kennedy's audacious goal, NASA successfully landed humans on the moon in 1969 through a $25 billion public investment. This monumental project required unprecedented national collaboration and drove rapid advancements in computing, materials science, and satellite technology that still benefit society today. It serves as powerful evidence that setting extraordinarily ambitious, government-funded goals can unify a nation and accelerate human progress.

**Core Lesson:** Audacious, government-funded goals can drive unprecedented technological innovation and national unity.

**Counter-Lesson:** Massive expenditures on space or abstract science can be heavily criticized when immediate terrestrial problems like poverty remain unsolved.

**Use For:** Government, science, big goals, public investment, competition, innovation, collaboration, space.

### E08 — P. C. Mahalanobis & Indian Statistical Planning
**Category:** Leadership / Policy
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** After India gained independence in 1947, the country faced severe poverty and lacked industrial infrastructure. Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis, a physicist and statistician who founded the Indian Statistical Institute (ISI), introduced rigorous statistical sampling and data collection methods to national planning. He formulated the Second Five-Year Plan (Mahalanobis Model), which prioritized heavy industrialization and public infrastructure based on mathematical and statistical models of the economy. His data-driven approach replaced arbitrary political policymaking with empirical economic planning, laying the foundation for India's modern industrial, scientific, and statistical infrastructure.

**Core Lesson:** Large-scale government policies and nation-building reforms are most effective when guided by empirical statistical data and rigorous mathematical planning rather than political rhetoric.

**Counter-Lesson:** Over-reliance on centralized mathematical models can ignore complex local realities, leading to bureaucratization and neglect of sectors like agriculture or consumer goods.

**Use For:** Reform, data-driven decisions, evidence-based policy, science in government, planning, structural reform.

### E09 — Elon Musk & SpaceX
**Category:** Innovation / Business
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Elon Musk risked his entire personal fortune to fund SpaceX, facing bankruptcy after three consecutive rocket failures before finally achieving orbit. By 2020, SpaceX became the first private company to send astronauts to the ISS and revolutionized the industry with reusable rockets that cut launch costs by 90%. His trajectory illustrates that radical, paradigm-shifting innovation requires immense risk tolerance, perseverance, and a willingness to embrace initial failure.

**Core Lesson:** Radical, industry-shifting innovation requires immense risk tolerance, long-term vision, and a willingness to embrace failure.

**Counter-Lesson:** High-risk, ego-driven leadership can lead to volatile business practices and significant personal or financial collateral damage.

**Use For:** Risk, innovation, entrepreneurship, technology, leadership, long-term thinking, perseverance.

### E10 — Marie Curie
**Category:** Science
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** Marie Curie discovered the elements radium and polonium, becoming the first person to win two Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields. She conducted her groundbreaking, foundational research under severe limitations, facing deep-seated sexism and being barred from higher education in her native Poland. Her obsessive dedication eventually revolutionized medical treatments through radiation therapy, proving that individual perseverance can overcome overwhelming systemic barriers.

**Core Lesson:** Unrelenting individual perseverance and passion can overcome severe systemic barriers and revolutionize human knowledge.

**Counter-Lesson:** Extreme dedication to one's work can come at massive personal cost (Curie ultimately died from radiation exposure related to her research).

**Use For:** Science, perseverance, gender barriers, education, individual genius, discovery, dedication.

### E11 — Nelson Mandela & Truth and Reconciliation
**Category:** Leadership / Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** After spending 27 years in prison as a political dissident, Nelson Mandela emerged to lead South Africa's fraught transition from apartheid to democracy. Rather than seeking retribution against his oppressors, he prioritized national unity by establishing the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. His moral leadership and willingness to compromise prevented a racial civil war, demonstrating that true leadership requires choosing long-term healing over immediate vengeance.

**Core Lesson:** True moral leadership often requires prioritizing long-term unity and principled compromise over justified vengeance.

**Counter-Lesson:** Compromise and reconciliation can sometimes allow perpetrators of horrific injustices to avoid full legal accountability.

**Use For:** Leadership, forgiveness, principle, compromise, justice, change, reconciliation, moral courage.

### E12 — Anand Kumar & Super 30 (2002)
**Category:** Education / Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** In 2002, mathematician Anand Kumar founded "Super 30" in Bihar, a program that selects 30 highly talented students from economically backward sections (children of cobblers, laborers, landless farmers) and coaches them for the hyper-competitive IIT-JEE exams. He provides free tuition, lodging, and food, funded entirely by his own earnings from running evening classes. Super 30 rejects the hyper-commercialized, rote-memorization "coaching factory" model of institutions like Kota, focusing instead on deep conceptual understanding, collaborative peer learning, and intrinsic motivation. The program has achieved near-100% success rates, showing that dedication and conceptual focus can beat commercialized education.

**Core Lesson:** True educational excellence and social mobility are achieved through conceptual depth, peer collaboration, and dedicated mentorship, proving that talent is not restricted by economic class.

**Counter-Lesson:** Small-scale, highly personalized mentorship models are extremely difficult to scale to a national level without losing quality or becoming institutionalized.

**Use For:** Education, student motivation, conceptual learning vs. rote memorization, social mobility, individual impact, commercialization of learning.

### E13 — Jio Digital Revolution (2016)
**Category:** Technology / Innovation
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In 2016, Reliance Jio entered the Indian telecom market by offering free voice calls and extremely cheap 4G data, triggering a price war that slashed data costs by 95%. This digital shock democratized internet access for hundreds of millions of low-income and rural Indians who had never used a computer, making India the world's largest consumer of mobile data. It sparked an economic revolution: enabling mobile banking (UPI), digital education, and empowering rural content creators. However, it also led to immediate challenges: the rapid spread of fake news on platforms like WhatsApp, digital addiction, and polarization, proving that rapid information democratization is a double-edged sword.

**Core Lesson:** A technological innovation that drastically lowers the cost of accessing information can democratize knowledge, bypass historical infrastructure deficits, and trigger massive economic development.

**Counter-Lesson:** The rapid, unregulated democratization of information can facilitate the spread of misinformation, cybercrimes, and social polarization, outpacing public digital literacy.

**Use For:** Technology, social change, democratization of knowledge, unintended consequences of innovation, rapid pace of life, digital access.

### E14 — Medici Family & the Renaissance
**Category:** Arts / Government
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** The powerful Medici banking family acted as the primary financial patrons for geniuses like Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Brunelleschi in 15th-century Florence. Their immense wealth bankrolled the creation of the Renaissance's greatest art and architecture, transforming their city into the cultural capital of the world. This historical precedent demonstrates that the flourishing of arts and culture often depends heavily on the financial backing of wealthy private patrons or institutions.

**Core Lesson:** The flourishing of high arts and enduring cultural heritage often depends entirely on concentrated financial patronage.

**Counter-Lesson:** When private wealth controls the arts, cultural output may be restricted to the tastes, politics, and vanity of the elite.

**Use For:** Arts funding, patronage, culture, creativity, government versus private, cultural heritage.

### E16 — Google's 20% Time
**Category:** Business / Innovation
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** Google famously implemented a policy allowing its engineers to spend 20% of their paid work hours pursuing personal, innovative projects unrelated to their core jobs. This unstructured creative freedom directly resulted in the creation of some of the company's most profitable products, including Gmail, Google Maps, and AdSense. The policy serves as a powerful business case that providing employees with autonomy and unstructured time yields far greater innovation than strict, top-down micromanagement.

**Core Lesson:** Unstructured time, creative autonomy, and trusting employees can yield breakthrough innovations that strict management cannot.

**Counter-Lesson:** Giving employees free reign requires hiring highly self-motivated individuals; otherwise, it can lead to wasted resources and lack of focus.

**Use For:** Innovation, business, creativity, education, motivation, structure versus freedom, intrapreneurship.

### E17 — India's Pulse Polio Initiative (1995–2014)
**Category:** Science / Public Health
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In the 1990s, India was widely considered the hardest place on Earth to eradicate polio due to high population density, poor sanitation, flooding, and vaccine hesitancy. In 1995, the government launched the Pulse Polio Initiative. This massive logistical campaign set up millions of booths, deployed health workers to travel door-to-door, and used mobile vaccination teams to immunize children at train stations and markets. The campaign mobilized celebrities, religious leaders, and local volunteers to fight vaccine hesitancy. Despite initial setbacks and doubts from global experts, India recorded its last wild polio case in 2011 and was certified polio-free in 2014, showing that public health science succeeds only when backed by relentless public coordination.

**Core Lesson:** Eradicating deep-seated systemic crises (like disease) requires combining scientific tools with relentless public mobilization, celebrity persuasion, and massive local coordination.

**Counter-Lesson:** Keeping a nation polio-free requires perpetual, high-cost surveillance and booster campaigns, which divert public resources from other urgent healthcare needs.

**Use For:** Public health, global/local cooperation, scientific implementation, overcoming logistical barriers, government campaigns.

### E18 — B. R. Ambedkar & the Indian Constitution
**Category:** Leadership / Government
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** As the Chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Indian Constitution, Dr. B. R. Ambedkar faced the task of shaping a legal framework for a deeply divided society fractured by caste, religion, and gender. Having faced severe untouchability and discrimination himself, Ambedkar prioritized human rights and social reform over traditional consensus. He drafted Article 17, which legally abolished untouchability and criminalized its practice, directly challenging centuries-old Hindu social dogmas. Despite intense opposition from conservative groups and traditionalists who argued that laws could not force social change, Ambedkar stood firm, using constitutional law as a tool to dismantle systemic oppression.

**Core Lesson:** True leadership requires the moral courage to use institutional authority and constitutional law to dismantle systemic injustices, even when it directly challenges centuries-old social traditions.

**Counter-Lesson:** Passing laws or constitutional articles against deep-seated social prejudices does not instantly erase them from society, showing that legal changes must be paired with cultural education to be fully effective.

**Use For:** Leadership, moral courage, law and society, government policy, social reform, challenging traditions, principle vs. popularity.

### E19 — Covaxin (Bharat Biotech)
**Category:** Science / Technology
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** During the COVID-19 pandemic, Indian firm Bharat Biotech developed "Covaxin," India's first indigenous vaccine, in collaboration with the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR). While Western developers focused on novel mRNA technology, Indian scientists chose an inactivated virus platform. Covaxin was developed, tested, and approved in under a year. This rapid response was only possible because of decades of prior, basic research on cell-culture platforms and vaccine manufacturing capabilities in India (e.g., developing vaccines for Rotavirus and Polio). It proved that rapid crisis response relies heavily on the quiet, long-term funding and preservation of domestic scientific capabilities.

**Core Lesson:** Rapid, successful crisis response relies on having established domestic scientific capabilities and long-term investments in basic research and infrastructure.

**Counter-Lesson:** Rapidly developing and approving vaccines under emergency use conditions can generate public skepticism and legal controversies regarding testing protocols, highlighting the need for absolute scientific transparency.

**Use For:** Science, technology, crisis response, government funding, basic research, domestic capability, speed.

### E20 — Jadav Payeng (The Forest Man of India)
**Category:** Science / Environment
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** In 1979, Jadav Payeng, a 16-year-old tribal boy in Assam, noticed a large number of snakes dying on a barren sandbar of the Brahmaputra River due to lack of shade. Over the next forty years, Payeng single-handedly planted and nurtured seeds and saplings on the sandbar. Today, that barren sandbar is the "Molai Forest"—a thriving ecosystem spanning 1,360 acres (larger than Central Park) that houses Bengal tigers, rhinos, elephants, and diverse bird species. Payeng had no formal education in forestry, botany, or environmental science. His achievement proved that an ordinary individual's dedication can restore complex ecosystems, outperforming well-funded government afforestation projects.

**Core Lesson:** Paradigm-shifting environmental restoration and scientific success can be achieved through individual passion and dedication, proving that formal credentials are not a prerequisite for making a massive impact.

**Counter-Lesson:** Individual environmental achievements are vulnerable to external threats like poaching, illegal logging, and lack of institutional protection, which require state enforcement to secure.

**Use For:** Environment, individual impact, passion, outsiders, expertise, forestry, conservation.

### E23 — Indira Gandhi ("Iron Lady of India")
**Category:** Leadership / Government
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** As Prime Minister of India, Indira Gandhi was known for her unyielding, iron-willed, and highly centralized leadership style. She pushed through massive, controversial reforms like the nationalization of 14 major private banks in 1969 and the abolition of privy purses for princely states, directly challenging the conservative elites and her own party's senior leadership. During the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War, she stood firm against intense geopolitical pressure, including threats from the US government (which sent its Seventh Fleet). While her tenure was highly polarizing and controversial (culminating in the authoritarian 21-month Emergency), she proved that executing structural reforms and managing geopolitical crises require a strong, uncompromising will.

**Core Lesson:** Driving structural changes and managing national crises require unyielding conviction-based leadership that is willing to endure political division and public hostility.

**Counter-Lesson:** Uncompromising, highly centralized leadership can bypass democratic checks and balances, leading to authoritarian excesses and severe political polarization.

**Use For:** Leadership, conviction-based leadership, government authority, structural reforms, crisis management, political division.

### E24 — A. R. Rahman
**Category:** Arts / Culture
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** A. R. Rahman revolutionized Indian cinema and popular music in the 1990s by introducing digital multi-track recording, synthesizers, and electronic arrangements to film scores. He broke traditional Bollywood formulas by blending Carnatic and Hindustani classical music with Western pop, electronic beats, Qawwali, and Sufi influences. Rahman collaborated with diverse vocalists, instrumentalists, and lyricists from across the world, pushing them to experiment and improvise. His unique compositions, such as his Oscar-winning work on *Slumdog Millionaire*, achieved global acclaim and permanently modernized the soundscape of South Asian music, showing that creative genius thrives on collaboration and constant stylistic experimentation.

**Core Lesson:** Creative brilliance and cultural impact flourish when artists relentlessly experiment across genres, collaborate with diverse talents, and refuse to rely on established formulas.

**Counter-Lesson:** Relentless experimentation and blending of diverse genres can alienate traditional audiences who prefer classical or familiar, established styles.

**Use For:** Arts, creativity, collaboration, innovation, cultural impact, experimentation.

### E25 — Aadhaar Biometric Identity System
**Category:** Technology / Policy
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In 2009, India launched Aadhaar, the world's largest biometric identity database, assigning a unique 12-digit number backed by fingerprints and iris scans to over 1.3 billion residents. Aadhaar revolutionized the delivery of government welfare benefits by eliminating duplicate/ghost identities, reducing corruption, and enabling instant digital transactions (UPI). However, it simultaneously generated intense ethical and legal controversies. Critics raised concerns about mass state surveillance, data security (leaks), and the exclusion of vulnerable citizens who failed biometric verification due to physical labor. The debate culminated in a landmark Supreme Court ruling that declared privacy a fundamental right, showing that massive technological breakthroughs are always double-edged swords.

**Core Lesson:** Large-scale technological innovations can dramatically improve efficiency and social welfare, but they simultaneously create profound ethical risks regarding privacy, data security, and systemic exclusion.

**Counter-Lesson:** Because technology develops faster than legal frameworks, governments and societies are often forced to write privacy laws retroactively to protect citizens from tech excesses.

**Use For:** Technology, policy, ethics, privacy, government intervention, unintended consequences of innovation, efficiency vs. security.

### 3. Counter-Lessons Cheat Sheet
*Use these to add nuance to your essay or to argue the opposite side of a prompt.*

- **If the prompt praises compromise:** Use **E23 (Indira)** or **E18 (Ambedkar)** to argue that compromise is sometimes cowardice, and true leadership requires unyielding conviction.
- **If the prompt praises expert authority:** Use **E20 (Payeng)** or **E21 (Wikipedia)** to argue that experts are often blinded by dogma, and outsiders or the crowd can be more accurate.
- **If the prompt praises technology/science unconditionally:** Use **E25 (Aadhaar)** to point out terrifying ethical dilemmas, or **E02 (Swaminathan)** to show that tech creates new environmental dependencies.
- **If the prompt demands pure empirical data:** Use **E05 (Jobs)** to argue that imagination, intuition, and aesthetics often beat pure data.

### 5. Essay Construction Blueprint
*How to pair examples for a nuanced, 5.0+ scoring essay:*

- **The "Macro/Micro" Blueprint:** Use one massive systemic example, and one deeply personal example. 
  *Example (Education):* Argue that schools should prioritize student interest. Paragraph 1 uses E12 (Anand Kumar) to prove it works on a national level. Paragraph 2 uses E24 (Rahman) or E16 (Google) to show how autonomy drives genius on a small-group level.
- **The "Past/Present" Blueprint:** Show that your argument applies across time.
  *Example (Technology):* Argue that tech causes disruption but ultimate progress. Paragraph 1 uses E13 (Jio) to show historical precedent. Paragraph 2 uses E21 (Wikipedia) or E19 (Covaxin) to show the modern equivalent.
- **The "Thesis/Antithesis" Blueprint (Nuance):**
  *Example (Leadership):* Argue that leaders must compromise, but not on core morals. Paragraph 1 uses E11 (Mandela) to show the necessity of compromise. Paragraph 2 uses E18 (Ambedkar) to define the absolute limit where compromise becomes immoral.

### 6. How to Use This Bank on Test Day
1. **Read the Prompt:** Identify the core tension (e.g., *Should leaders listen to the public or follow their own principles?*).
2. **Pick Your Stance:** E.g., *Leaders must follow principles, but communicate them well.*
3. **Select 2 Examples:** Instantly pull from your Top 10. E.g., E18 (Ambedkar - Principle) and E11 (Mandela - Nuance/Compromise).
4. **Draft the Argument (NOT the fact):** 
   - *Bad:* "B. R. Ambedkar was the father of the Indian Constitution who outlawed untouchability."
   - *Good:* "When faced with intense social opposition, B. R. Ambedkar drafted Article 17 of the Indian Constitution to abolish untouchability, proving that true leadership requires using legal authority to prioritize moral imperatives over traditional consensus."
5. **Analyze:** Add the "Core Lesson" to the end of your paragraph. Connect it explicitly back to the prompt's wording.

### 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid
- **Fact-Dropping (The #1 Mistake):** The GRE graders do not care if you know what year the Vaccine Maitri initiative happened. They care *why it matters*. Spend 2 sentences explaining the *what*, and 3 sentences explaining the *why*.
- **Forcing a Bad Fit:** If a prompt is about "Arts funding," do not try to twist the Apollo Moon Landing into an art project. Just use E14 (Medici) or E24 (Rahman).
- **Ignoring the Nuance:** If the prompt says "Always" or "Never," your essay must acknowledge exceptions. Use the "Counter-Lessons Cheat Sheet" to write a devastatingly smart concession paragraph. (e.g., "While Dabbawalas prove decentralized systems work, we must concede that without local rail infrastructure, they cannot scale...")
- **Using 5 Examples poorly:** A 5.0+ essay only needs **two** highly developed examples (one per body paragraph). Do not list off 5 examples in a shallow way. Deep, analytical paragraphs score higher than lists.
