# GRE Issue Essay — Example Bank, Themes & Prompt Mapping

> Personal notes on examples, themes, and argument patterns for GRE Issue Essays. The aim is to build a flexible set of examples that can be adapted across a wide range of prompts while maintaining clear reasoning and relevant analysis.

---

## PART 1: THE 25 EXAMPLES

### E01 — Marshall Plan (1948)
**Category:** Government / International
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** After World War II, the United States invested $13 billion (over $150 billion today) to rebuild Western European economies. Instead of punishing former enemies, this strategic generosity stabilized democracies, created reliable long-term trade partners, and successfully contained the spread of communism. While critics at the time condemned it as an impossibly expensive giveaway, it proved to be an essential public investment that secured decades of geopolitical stability and economic growth.

**Core Lesson:** Short-term financial sacrifice and strategic generosity often yield massive long-term stability and economic growth.

**Counter-Lesson:** Massive government spending can be viewed as wasteful interventionism; success requires the recipient to be capable of utilizing the aid effectively.

**Use For:** Government spending, international cooperation, long-term thinking, public investment, foreign aid, crisis response, economic policy.

### E02 — Norman Borlaug & Green Revolution
**Category:** Science / Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Norman Borlaug developed high-yield, disease-resistant wheat varieties that doubled food production in developing nations, directly saving over one billion lives from starvation. His scientific innovations transformed agriculture across Mexico, India, and Pakistan, averting the mass famines predicted in the 1960s. He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970, demonstrating that applied science can solve catastrophic social problems when paired with global cooperation.

**Core Lesson:** Applied science and technological innovation can solve catastrophic social problems and save millions of lives.

**Counter-Lesson:** Rapid technological shifts in agriculture can lead to environmental degradation and economic dependencies on synthetic fertilizers.

**Use For:** Science solving problems, public funding, individual impact, global cooperation, agriculture, technology for good.

---

### E03 — Alan Turing & Enigma Codebreaking
**Category:** Science / Technology
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** During WWII, Alan Turing cracked Nazi Germany's "unbreakable" Enigma code by conceptualizing early computing machines and applying abstract mathematics to a practical crisis. His interdisciplinary approach fundamentally shortened the war by an estimated two years and saved millions of lives. Furthermore, as an eccentric outsider who faced severe persecution, his story proves that groundbreaking innovation often comes from unconventional thinkers who challenge established methods.

**Core Lesson:** Groundbreaking innovation often requires interdisciplinary thinking and unconventional outsiders challenging established norms.

**Counter-Lesson:** Pure intellect and technological advancement do not guarantee personal acceptance or ethical treatment by society.

**Use For:** Science, innovation, outsiders, war, technology, interdisciplinary work, genius, challenging authority.

---

### E04 — Muhammad Yunus & Grameen Bank
**Category:** Social Reform / Business
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Muhammad Yunus pioneered the concept of microfinance by lending tiny sums of money to impoverished women in rural Bangladesh who lacked traditional collateral. His Grameen Bank lifted millions out of extreme poverty by trusting the poor to be entrepreneurial and reliable borrowers. By proving that a bank could be both socially responsible and financially self-sustaining, Yunus demonstrated that capitalism can be harnessed for profound grassroots social reform.

**Core Lesson:** Grassroots empowerment and socially responsible capitalism can effectively eradicate systemic poverty.

**Counter-Lesson:** Profit-driven motives can sometimes distort social missions if microfinance becomes predatory or interest rates rise too high.

**Use For:** Social responsibility, business, poverty, innovation, individual impact, capitalism with conscience, grassroots.

---

### E05 — Steve Jobs & Apple
**Category:** Business / Innovation
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** Steve Jobs transformed multiple industries—including personal computing, music, and telecommunications—by obsessing over design, user experience, and aesthetic perfection rather than just technical functionality. His insistence that technology should be intuitive and beautiful resulted in the iPhone, which redefined modern life. His ouster and triumphant return to Apple underline that true visionary leadership requires a balance of uncompromising creative vision and effective execution.

**Core Lesson:** Visionary leadership and an uncompromising focus on user experience often triumph over pure technical utility.

**Counter-Lesson:** Autocratic and demanding leadership styles can alienate colleagues, showing the friction between individual genius and teamwork.

**Use For:** Innovation, leadership, business, technology, vision, creative thinking, competition, design.

---

### E06 — Rosa Parks & Civil Rights Movement
**Category:** Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** In 1955, Rosa Parks famously refused to surrender her bus seat to a white passenger in Montgomery, Alabama, sparking a 381-day transit boycott. Her single, calculated act of civil disobedience served as the catalyst for the broader American Civil Rights Movement. Her story is the ultimate proof that ordinary individuals, armed with unyielding moral conviction, can trigger extraordinary, systemic social change and overturn unjust laws.

**Core Lesson:** A single act of principled civil disobedience by an ordinary individual can catalyze massive, systemic social change.

**Counter-Lesson:** Individual actions require the backing of organized, collective movements (like the subsequent boycott) to achieve lasting, legislative reform.

**Use For:** Individual action, social reform, justice, courage, grassroots change, civil disobedience, moral conviction.

### E07 — Apollo 11 Moon Landing
**Category:** Government / Science
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Fulfilling President Kennedy's audacious goal, NASA successfully landed humans on the moon in 1969 through a $25 billion public investment. This monumental project required unprecedented national collaboration and drove rapid advancements in computing, materials science, and satellite technology that still benefit society today. It serves as powerful evidence that setting extraordinarily ambitious, government-funded goals can unify a nation and accelerate human progress.

**Core Lesson:** Audacious, government-funded goals can drive unprecedented technological innovation and national unity.

**Counter-Lesson:** Massive expenditures on space or abstract science can be heavily criticized when immediate terrestrial problems like poverty remain unsolved.

**Use For:** Government, science, big goals, public investment, competition, innovation, collaboration, space.

### E08 — Florence Nightingale
**Category:** Leadership / Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** Florence Nightingale revolutionized the nursing profession during the Crimean War by introducing rigorous sanitation practices and data-tracking to military hospitals. By meticulously gathering statistics, she proved that more soldiers were dying from preventable infections than from battle wounds, dropping the mortality rate from 42% to 2%. Her legacy proves that even traditionally compassionate, care-based fields must rely on empirical data and strict methodology to be truly effective.

**Core Lesson:** Compassionate fields and social policies are most effective when driven by rigorous empirical data and scientific methodology.

**Counter-Lesson:** A strict reliance on data and rigid rules can sometimes clash with the need for personalized, empathetic human care.

**Use For:** Reform, data-driven decisions, leadership, women, perseverance, statistics, evidence-based policy.

### E09 — Elon Musk & SpaceX
**Category:** Innovation / Business
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Elon Musk risked his entire personal fortune to fund SpaceX, facing bankruptcy after three consecutive rocket failures before finally achieving orbit. By 2020, SpaceX became the first private company to send astronauts to the ISS and revolutionized the industry with reusable rockets that cut launch costs by 90%. His trajectory illustrates that radical, paradigm-shifting innovation requires immense risk tolerance, perseverance, and a willingness to embrace initial failure.

**Core Lesson:** Radical, industry-shifting innovation requires immense risk tolerance, long-term vision, and a willingness to embrace failure.

**Counter-Lesson:** High-risk, ego-driven leadership can lead to volatile business practices and significant personal or financial collateral damage.

**Use For:** Risk, innovation, entrepreneurship, technology, leadership, long-term thinking, perseverance.

### E10 — Marie Curie
**Category:** Science
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** Marie Curie discovered the elements radium and polonium, becoming the first person to win two Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields. She conducted her groundbreaking, foundational research under severe limitations, facing deep-seated sexism and being barred from higher education in her native Poland. Her obsessive dedication eventually revolutionized medical treatments through radiation therapy, proving that individual perseverance can overcome overwhelming systemic barriers.

**Core Lesson:** Unrelenting individual perseverance and passion can overcome severe systemic barriers and revolutionize human knowledge.

**Counter-Lesson:** Extreme dedication to one's work can come at massive personal cost (Curie ultimately died from radiation exposure related to her research).

**Use For:** Science, perseverance, gender barriers, education, individual genius, discovery, dedication.

### E11 — Nelson Mandela & Truth and Reconciliation
**Category:** Leadership / Social Reform
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** After spending 27 years in prison as a political dissident, Nelson Mandela emerged to lead South Africa's fraught transition from apartheid to democracy. Rather than seeking retribution against his oppressors, he prioritized national unity by establishing the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. His moral leadership and willingness to compromise prevented a racial civil war, demonstrating that true leadership requires choosing long-term healing over immediate vengeance.

**Core Lesson:** True moral leadership often requires prioritizing long-term unity and principled compromise over justified vengeance.

**Counter-Lesson:** Compromise and reconciliation can sometimes allow perpetrators of horrific injustices to avoid full legal accountability.

**Use For:** Leadership, forgiveness, principle, compromise, justice, change, reconciliation, moral courage.

### E12 — Finland's Education Reforms
**Category:** Education
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Finland fundamentally reformed its education system by abolishing standardized testing, school rankings, and intensive homework, instead prioritizing student well-being and teacher autonomy. Despite ignoring the hyper-competitive models used globally, Finnish students consistently rank at the top of international assessments. This systemic success proves that cooperation, equity, and intrinsic motivation are far more effective drivers of learning than rigid competition and rote memorization.

**Core Lesson:** Educational excellence is best achieved through cooperation, teacher autonomy, and student well-being, rather than rigid competition.

**Counter-Lesson:** Homogeneous, well-funded societies can implement reforms that might not scale directly to highly diverse, unequal populations.

**Use For:** Education, reform, competition in learning, policy, testing, teaching, student motivation.

### E13 — Gutenberg Printing Press (c. 1450)
**Category:** Technology / Innovation
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** Johannes Gutenberg's invention of the mechanical movable-type printing press drastically reduced the cost of books, effectively ending the Church's monopoly on information. By making knowledge widely accessible, the press directly enabled the Protestant Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, and the widespread democratization of literacy. It stands as the quintessential example of how a single technological innovation can inadvertently trigger centuries of profound, compounding social and intellectual progress.

**Core Lesson:** A single technological innovation that democratizes information can trigger centuries of compounding social and intellectual progress.

**Counter-Lesson:** The rapid spread of information can also facilitate the rapid spread of propaganda, division, and social unrest.

**Use For:** Technology, social change, knowledge, innovation, unintended consequences, democratisation.

### E14 — Medici Family & the Renaissance
**Category:** Arts / Government
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** The powerful Medici banking family acted as the primary financial patrons for geniuses like Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Brunelleschi in 15th-century Florence. Their immense wealth bankrolled the creation of the Renaissance's greatest art and architecture, transforming their city into the cultural capital of the world. This historical precedent demonstrates that the flourishing of arts and culture often depends heavily on the financial backing of wealthy private patrons or institutions.

**Core Lesson:** The flourishing of high arts and enduring cultural heritage often depends entirely on concentrated financial patronage.

**Counter-Lesson:** When private wealth controls the arts, cultural output may be restricted to the tastes, politics, and vanity of the elite.

**Use For:** Arts funding, patronage, culture, creativity, government versus private, cultural heritage.

### E15 — Paris Climate Agreement (2015)
**Category:** Government / Environment
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** In a historic diplomatic achievement, nearly 200 nations signed the Paris Agreement, pledging collective action to limit global warming and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. While the agreement is heavily criticized for being non-binding and lacking strict enforcement mechanisms, it marks an unprecedented moment of global consensus regarding a shared existential threat. It illustrates both the necessity of international cooperation for environmental crises and the inherent weakness of global governance.

**Core Lesson:** Addressing global, existential threats requires unprecedented international cooperation and collective consensus.

**Counter-Lesson:** International agreements without binding enforcement mechanisms are often symbolic and easily ignored when economic interests conflict.

**Use For:** Environment, international cooperation, government, policy, collective action, long-term threats.

### E16 — Google's 20% Time
**Category:** Business / Innovation
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** Google famously implemented a policy allowing its engineers to spend 20% of their paid work hours pursuing personal, innovative projects unrelated to their core jobs. This unstructured creative freedom directly resulted in the creation of some of the company's most profitable products, including Gmail, Google Maps, and AdSense. The policy serves as a powerful business case that providing employees with autonomy and unstructured time yields far greater innovation than strict, top-down micromanagement.

**Core Lesson:** Unstructured time, creative autonomy, and trusting employees can yield breakthrough innovations that strict management cannot.

**Counter-Lesson:** Giving employees free reign requires hiring highly self-motivated individuals; otherwise, it can lead to wasted resources and lack of focus.

**Use For:** Innovation, business, creativity, education, motivation, structure versus freedom, intrapreneurship.

### E17 — Global Polio Eradication Campaign
**Category:** Science / Global Cooperation
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** Through a massive, coordinated effort by Rotary International, the WHO, and local governments, global polio cases have been reduced by 99.9% since 1988, saving an estimated 18 million people from paralysis. This decades-long initiative required navigating war zones, cultural resistance, and logistical nightmares to deliver vaccines to the world's most remote children. It proves that combining scientific advancement with relentless, long-term global cooperation can literally eradicate diseases.

**Core Lesson:** Relentless, long-term global cooperation paired with scientific advancement can overcome immense logistical barriers to save millions.

**Counter-Lesson:** The final 0.1% of eradication is exponentially harder due to political instability, showing the limits of science in the face of human conflict.

**Use For:** Science, global cooperation, public health, persistence, long-term goals, vaccination.

### E18 — Abraham Lincoln & Emancipation Proclamation
**Category:** Leadership / Government
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In the midst of the bloody American Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation in 1863, declaring slaves in Confederate territory to be free. He made this monumentally controversial decision based on deep moral principle, despite facing intense political backlash, a divided cabinet, and the risk of losing the war. Lincoln's actions define the essence of true leadership: the courage to prioritize ethical imperatives over public popularity or political safety.

**Core Lesson:** True leadership requires the moral courage to prioritize ethical imperatives over public popularity or political safety.

**Counter-Lesson:** Acting on strict moral principle can deeply polarize a nation and require fighting a devastating war to enforce the new paradigm.

**Use For:** Leadership, principle versus popularity, government, moral courage, change, conviction.

### E19 — COVID-19 mRNA Vaccines
**Category:** Science / Technology
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** In response to the global pandemic, pharmaceutical companies like Moderna and Pfizer developed highly effective COVID-19 vaccines in under a year—a process that historically took 5 to 10 years. This unprecedented speed was only possible because governments heavily subsidized the financial risk and because scientists had spent decades conducting obscure, fundamental research on mRNA technology. This proves that rapid crisis response relies heavily on the quiet, long-term funding of basic scientific research.

**Core Lesson:** Rapid, miraculous technological responses to crises are only possible due to decades of prior investment in basic, theoretical research.

**Counter-Lesson:** Extremely rapid scientific advancement can generate public skepticism and fear, highlighting the need for transparent communication alongside innovation.

**Use For:** Science, technology, speed, public health, government funding, crisis response, basic research.

### E20 — Jane Goodall
**Category:** Science
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** Arriving in Gombe with no formal scientific training, Jane Goodall revolutionized primatology by patiently observing chimpanzees using tools—a behavior previously thought to be exclusively human. Her unconventional approach of living among her subjects and giving them names defied the rigid, detached scientific dogma of the era. Her groundbreaking discoveries prove that passion, patience, and a willingness to challenge established academic authority can profoundly expand human knowledge.

**Core Lesson:** Unconventional methods, passion, and challenging established academic dogma can lead to paradigm-shifting discoveries.

**Counter-Lesson:** Lack of formal training can sometimes lead to methodological errors or anthropomorphism, which traditional science seeks to avoid.

**Use For:** Science, outsiders, passion, perseverance, challenging authority, discovery, unconventional paths.

### E21 — Wikipedia
**Category:** Technology
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** Wikipedia was created as a radical experiment: a free, comprehensive encyclopedia written entirely by anonymous, unpaid volunteers. Despite initial mockery from academics who believed crowdsourced information would be inherently unreliable, studies have repeatedly shown Wikipedia's accuracy rivals that of traditional, expert-written resources like Britannica. It stands as modern proof that decentralized collaboration and the democratization of knowledge can successfully challenge traditional, centralized authority.

**Core Lesson:** Decentralized, crowdsourced collaboration can produce resources that rival or exceed traditional, centralized expert systems.

**Counter-Lesson:** Open systems are vulnerable to bias, vandalism, and misinformation, requiring constant vigilance and community moderation to maintain integrity.

**Use For:** Technology, collaboration, crowdsourcing, knowledge, authority, expertise, democratisation.

### E22 — Amul Dairy Cooperative (India)
**Category:** Social Reform / Business
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** Founded in 1946, the Amul cooperative organized marginalized Indian dairy farmers to bypass exploitative middlemen and sell their milk directly to the market. By pooling their resources, the farmers broke oppressive corporate cartels, ensured fair wages, and ultimately transformed India into the world's largest milk producer. Amul demonstrates that grassroots collective action and cooperative business models can successfully outcompete traditional corporate structures while driving massive social uplift.

**Core Lesson:** Grassroots collective action and cooperative business models can break monopolies and drive massive socioeconomic development.

**Counter-Lesson:** Cooperatives can become sluggish or overly bureaucratic as they scale, sometimes struggling to adapt as quickly as private enterprises.

**Use For:** Social reform, business, grassroots, collective action, poverty, development, cooperation.

### E23 — Margaret Thatcher
**Category:** Leadership / Government
**Strength Rating:** 8/10

**Description:** As Britain's first female Prime Minister, Margaret Thatcher fundamentally transformed the UK's struggling economy through aggressive privatization, deregulation, and the breaking of powerful labor unions. She held absolute, unyielding firm to her free-market convictions, famously declaring "the lady's not for turning" despite intense public hatred and widespread protests. Her tenure exemplifies conviction-based leadership, demonstrating that driving necessary structural reform often requires an iron will and a willingness to be deeply unpopular.

**Core Lesson:** Driving necessary but painful structural reform requires conviction-based leadership and a willingness to endure intense public opposition.

**Counter-Lesson:** Unyielding, uncompromising leadership can severely fracture society and leave vulnerable populations marginalized in the pursuit of economic efficiency.

**Use For:** Leadership, conviction, government, reform, economics, principle versus compromise.

### E24 — The Beatles
**Category:** Arts / Culture
**Strength Rating:** 7/10

**Description:** The Beatles revolutionized the trajectory of popular music by relentlessly experimenting with new studio techniques and blending rock with classical, Indian, and avant-garde influences. Remarkably, their cultural dominance and unmatched body of work were produced during a staggeringly brief, high-pressure ten-year collaboration. Their unparalleled success proves that true creative brilliance flourishes in environments of intense collaboration, mutual competition, and a constant refusal to rely on past formulas.

**Core Lesson:** Unparalleled creative brilliance flourishes through intense collaboration, continuous experimentation, and a refusal to stagnate.

**Counter-Lesson:** Intense creative partnerships are often volatile and unsustainable long-term due to clashing egos and evolving individual interests.

**Use For:** Arts, creativity, collaboration, leadership, cultural impact, innovation, teamwork.

### E25 — CRISPR Gene Editing (Doudna & Charpentier)
**Category:** Science / Ethics
**Strength Rating:** 9/10

**Description:** Scientists Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier developed CRISPR-Cas9, a revolutionary tool that allows for cheap, highly precise editing of DNA. While CRISPR holds the miraculous potential to cure genetic diseases and engineer resilient crops, it simultaneously raises profound, terrifying ethical questions regarding "designer babies" and irreversible ecological changes. It is the ultimate modern example of how monumental scientific breakthroughs inevitably create dual-edged swords of unprecedented opportunity and complex moral dilemmas.

**Core Lesson:** Monumental scientific breakthroughs inevitably bring both unprecedented opportunities and profound, complex moral dilemmas.

**Counter-Lesson:** Because technology evolves faster than ethical frameworks or government regulations, scientific capability often outpaces our wisdom to use it safely.

**Use For:** Science, ethics, innovation, regulation, unintended consequences, discovery, risk.

### 5. Essay Construction Blueprint
*How to pair examples for a nuanced, 5.0+ scoring essay:*

- **The "Macro/Micro" Blueprint:** Use one massive systemic example, and one deeply personal example. 
  *Example (Education):* Argue that schools should prioritize student interest. Paragraph 1 uses E12 (Finland) to prove it works on a national level. Paragraph 2 uses E24 (Beatles) or E16 (Google) to show how autonomy drives genius on a small-group level.
- **The "Past/Present" Blueprint:** Show that your argument applies across time.
  *Example (Technology):* Argue that tech causes disruption but ultimate progress. Paragraph 1 uses E13 (Gutenberg) to show historical precedent. Paragraph 2 uses E21 (Wikipedia) or E19 (mRNA) to show the modern equivalent.
- **The "Thesis/Antithesis" Blueprint (Nuance):**
  *Example (Leadership):* Argue that leaders must compromise, but not on core morals. Paragraph 1 uses E11 (Mandela) to show the necessity of compromise. Paragraph 2 uses E18 (Lincoln) to define the absolute limit where compromise becomes immoral.

### 6. How to Use This Bank on Test Day
1. **Read the Prompt:** Identify the core tension (e.g., *Should leaders listen to the public or follow their own principles?*).
2. **Pick Your Stance:** E.g., *Leaders must follow principles, but communicate them well.*
3. **Select 2 Examples:** Instantly pull from your Top 10. E.g., E18 (Lincoln - Principle) and E11 (Mandela - Nuance/Compromise).
4. **Draft the Argument (NOT the fact):** 
   - *Bad:* "Abraham Lincoln was the president during the Civil War and he freed the slaves."
   - *Good:* "When faced with intense political opposition, Abraham Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation, proving that true leadership requires prioritizing moral imperatives over immediate public approval."
5. **Analyze:** Add the "Core Lesson" to the end of your paragraph. Connect it explicitly back to the prompt's wording.

### 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid
- **Fact-Dropping (The #1 Mistake):** The GRE graders do not care if you know what year the Marshall Plan happened. They care *why it matters*. Spend 2 sentences explaining the *what*, and 3 sentences explaining the *why*.
- **Forcing a Bad Fit:** If a prompt is about "Arts funding," do not try to twist the Apollo Moon Landing into an art project. Just use E14 (Medici) or E24 (Beatles).
- **Ignoring the Nuance:** If the prompt says "Always" or "Never," your essay must acknowledge exceptions. Use the "Counter-Lessons Cheat Sheet" to write a devastatingly smart concession paragraph. (e.g., "While Wikipedia proves crowdsourcing works, we must concede that without moderation, it devolves into misinformation...")
- **Using 5 Examples poorly:** A 5.0+ essay only needs **two** highly developed examples (one per body paragraph). Do not list off 5 examples in a shallow way. Deep, analytical paragraphs score higher than lists.
